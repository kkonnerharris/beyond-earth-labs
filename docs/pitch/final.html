THE PROBLEM

A crew leaving the habitat has three questions.

01 · PLAN

Where can the crew go?

A route on measured terrain that stays under the slope a rover or a suited walker can cross.

02 · CHECK

Can it get back?

Oxygen or battery for the whole trip with a reserve, and a rule that the way home is always affordable.

03 · RUN

What if the plan breaks?

An obstacle, a dust storm, a suit leak or a health alert forces a new plan, and the log says why.

THE MAP

Five NASA missions on one map of Jezero Crater.

Imagery. Orbital photographs at 6 m and 25 cm, a global colour mosaic, and thermal infrared.

Elevation. A 20 m model for planning and a 1 m model for checking. Slope and roughness are calculated from them.

The real rover. The white line is where Perseverance drove. Click near it for the rover’s own camera frames.

Checked against that rover. The map’s elevation agrees with Perseverance’s record at 464 waypoints to a median of 1.0 m. It never crossed ground steeper than 18.3°, which is where the 20° rover limit comes from.

Labelled. Every layer and number is tagged NASA data, calculated, model, simulated or assumption.

The mission map. Seven steps on the left carry one mission from the map to a review. The orange line is a planned traverse to a science target on the delta front; the white line is Perseverance’s real path.

BEYOND EARTH LABS03 / 10

HOW IT WORKS

A Marswalk in three steps: plan, check, run.

01 · PLAN

Where can the crew go?

The route is computed on NASA elevation data for a crew rover, a scout rover or an astronaut on foot. Each science stop adds time that counts against supplies.

02 · CHECK

Can it get back safely?

The trip is checked against oxygen or battery, with a reserve. A plan that does not fit is refused, and the shortfall is shown.

03 · RUN

What if the plan breaks?

The traverse runs as a simulation. Emergencies force a new plan, a habitat budget follows the mission, and a finished traverse is scored.

CHECKED AT 1 METRE

The 20 m plan cannot see a steep bank. NASA’s 1 m model can.

A route planned on 20 m squares is measured again on the 1 m HiRISE elevation model, reading only the elevation under the route. On the traverse to the delta front shown here:

9.5°

What the 20 m plan reports as the steepest ground on the route. The crew rover’s limit is 20°.

152 m

What the 1 m model finds: nine stretches steeper than the limit, up to 41.8°.

0 m

After the route is moved in two replans. Its steepest point is now 18.0°, and it is 656 m longer.

Not every route can be cleared. To the top of the delta, 46 m of steep ground remains out of 258 m, and the check says so.

The Route step after the check. Red marks the ground the 1 m model shows as too steep, which the moved route now keeps out of. Below the summary: the slope along the route against the 20° limit.

BEYOND EARTH LABS06 / 10

THE 3D SIMULATION · EARTH

How a test on Earth could help before anyone goes.

Mars hardware and procedures are tried on Earth first. The same rover, astronaut and planner run at Garden of the Gods in Shawnee National Forest, Illinois, on USGS elevation and a real aerial photograph. On ground a person can walk, a test could show:

Whether the predictions hold. Walk or drive a planned route and compare the time and energy with what the planner said.

Whether the limits are right. Take the 20° rule to a real slope, and see what the map missed: rocks, roots and loose ground smaller than a metre.

Whether the rules work under pressure. Rehearse the turn-back for a leak or a storm, with the way home always covered.

No field test has been run yet. This site is a practice ground near home, not one the network ranked, and Earth’s gravity and forest are not Mars.

BEYOND EARTH LABS07 / 10

DEEP LEARNING

A terrain network finds Earth sites that resemble Mars.

A vision transformer with a head trained on 4,534 terrain cells from three worlds ranks 29 Earth regions against any Mars or Moon region. Every answer is checked against measured elevation.

0.64

Mars regions it never saw. It explains 64% of the variation in slope, relief and roughness. Classical methods reach 52%, a simple linear model 60%.

52%

Matching Earth terrain to Mars. It closes 52% of the gap between a random pick and the best possible pick. Untrained, 17%.

0.39

Weakest on Earth. Vegetation hides the landform. An Earth-only specialist explains 39% on Earth regions it never saw; the shared network, 31%.

The network only ranks rehearsal sites. Routes never depend on it.

The top match for Jezero Crater is the Mojave volcanic fields, with the network’s score and the measured score side by side.

AI USE AND STATUS

How AI was used, what works today, and what does not.

HOW AI WAS USED

To build it. Claude (Anthropic), through Claude Code, for project scaffolding, for choosing and testing which NASA data to use, and as a coding assistant on the code, tests and documents.

Inside the product. DINOv2 as a frozen image encoder, and a small terrain network trained by this project on NASA data.

Not used for numbers. No language model produces any figure shown. Routes, risks and budgets come from documented calculations.

WORKS TODAY

All seven steps run end to end, and the 3D simulation runs the mission on Mars and a rover and astronaut on Earth.

The elevation map agrees with Perseverance’s own record to a median of 1.0 m, at 464 waypoints.

112 unit tests and 27 end-to-end checks pass, and a 24-screen walkthrough of the site runs with no errors.

KNOWN LIMITS

Routes are planned at 20 m and checked at 1 m. Single rocks and ripples are still invisible.

The traverse and the crew health readings are simulated. Only 2 km around the landing site is detailed in 3D.

Some life-support, suit and radiation figures are assumptions, flagged for checking. No field test on Earth has been run.
